How much does it cost to charge an electric car at home?

Short answer: about 2p a mile on an overnight EV tariff, and about 8.4p a mile if you pay the standard price cap. For a typical 7,000 miles a year that's roughly £150 against £585. Below are the real numbers for popular cars, how to work out your own, and what January's price cap rise does to them.

The simple formula

Every home charging cost comes down to three numbers: how much energy the car needs, how much is lost on the way in, and what you pay per kWh.

Cost = kWh into the battery ÷ 0.9 × your rate per kWh.

The ÷ 0.9 allows for about 10% lost as heat in the charger, cable and battery, which you pay for at the meter. So putting 40 kWh into a battery means buying about 44 kWh from the grid. At the 26.32p price cap that's £11.70; at 6.66p it's £2.96.

To find your rate, look at your latest bill or app for the unit rate in pence per kWh. If you're on a two-rate tariff, use the off-peak rate for anything you charge overnight and the day rate for the rest.

The gap is striking. The same 7,000 miles cost about £150 charged overnight at home and about £1,640 charged entirely at rapid chargers: more than ten times as much. Most drivers mix the two. With 80% of charging at home on a 6.66p tariff and 20% at rapid chargers, the year comes to about £446, or £37 a month.

If you drive more or less, scale it: every 1,000 miles costs about £21 on a 6.66p tariff and about £84 on the price cap. For your exact car and mileage, use the EV charging cost calculator.

What January's price cap means for home charging

Ofgem confirmed on 25 November 2026 that the price cap for 1 January to 31 March 2027 rises to 32.69p per kWh, up from 26.32p, with the standing charge almost unchanged at 54.77p a day. That means charging on a standard variable tariff goes from about 8.4p to 10.4p a mile, or roughly £141 more a year for 7,000 miles.

Two things soften the blow. Electricity stays VAT-free until 30 June 2027, which is already included in the new rate. And fixed EV tariffs don't follow the cap: if you fix now, your overnight rate stays put until the fix ends. The cheapest current EV rates are still about a quarter of even today's cap, so the case for switching gets stronger, not weaker, in January.

Five things that change your real cost

  • Your tariff. By far the biggest factor. An EV tariff can cut the cost of each mile by about 75% against the cap. Compare them side by side on our EV tariff comparison, or read the cheapest way to charge at home.
  • The day rate. EV tariffs charge more than the cap in the day: MoneySavingExpert lists average peak rates of about 33–42p. If you use a lot of electricity in the daytime, that eats into the saving. British Gas EV Power is a good example; see our British Gas EV tariff guide.
  • Your charger. A 7 kW wallbox adds about 31 kWh in a five-hour cheap window, roughly 110 miles. A 3-pin plug at about 2.3 kW adds only about 11 kWh in the same time, so the rest spills into peak hours.
  • Winter. Cold weather and running the heater lower efficiency, so the same miles need noticeably more kWh between December and February. Pre-heat the car while it's still plugged in.
  • The standing charge. You pay it whether you charge or not (54.83p a day on the cap), so it isn't part of the cost of charging. But compare it when you switch, because it varies by tariff and region.

Is home charging always cheaper?

Almost always. Even on the price cap, home charging at 26.32p is about half the cost of the average slower public charger (54p) and about a third of a rapid charger (82p). The exceptions are free charging at work or a supermarket, and Tesla drivers using Superchargers, where Zapmap puts the average price paid by Tesla owners at 48p per kWh. That is still well above any home rate.

If you can't charge at home, look at lamp-post and on-street chargers, workplace charging and network subscriptions, which lower the per-kWh price below the pay-as-you-go averages above.

Questions

How much does it cost to charge an electric car at home in the UK?

On the standard price cap (26.32p per kWh until 31 December 2026) about 8.4p a mile, or roughly £585 a year for 7,000 miles. On an overnight EV tariff at 6.5–6.66p it is about 2p a mile, roughly £145–£150 a year.

How much does a full charge cost at home?

Multiply the usable battery size by your rate and add about 10% for charging losses. A 52 kWh Renault 5 costs about £15.21 from empty to full on the price cap and about £3.85 on EDF GoElectric's 6.66p overnight rate.

How much does it cost to charge an electric car per month?

For 7,000 miles a year in a car doing 3.5 miles per kWh: about £12 a month on a 6.5–6.66p EV tariff, £17 at 9.4p, and about £49 a month on the price cap.

Will charging at home cost more from January 2027?

Yes, if you are on a standard variable tariff. Ofgem confirmed the cap rises to 32.69p per kWh from 1 January to 31 March 2027, which lifts the price-cap cost to about 10.4p a mile. Fixed EV tariffs are not affected until they end.

Is charging at home cheaper than a public charger?

Much cheaper. Zapmap's October 2026 averages are 54p per kWh for slower public chargers and 82p for rapid ones, three to twelve times a home rate. Rapid charging works out at about 23p a mile.
